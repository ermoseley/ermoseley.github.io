Shocks

The background on the front page is incompressible — a projection method, an elliptic pressure, no shocks anywhere by construction. This is the other half of the subject: the isothermal Euler equations, solved conservatively with a Godunov scheme, driven into supersonic turbulence, running live in this tab.

What the solver actually is

Conserved state is (ρ, ρu, ρv) on a periodic, square-celled grid. Isothermal closure: P = ρcs2, with cs = 1, so every velocity printed on this page is a Mach number. That closure is not a shortcut — it is the standard approximation for the cold neutral and molecular ISM, where line cooling pins the temperature over a dynamical time. It also buys a constant sound speed, which makes the CFL condition trivial and honest, and it fits the state in three channels instead of four.

reconstruct
None. Piecewise constant — the interface states are just the cell averages. So this is first-order Godunov: no slopes, no limiter, no predictor half-step. It is the cheapest thing that still resolves a shock, and the diffusiveness that costs is visible as slightly fat shock fronts.
flux
HLL, with RAMSES's wave-speed estimate rather than plain Davis: SL = min(min(uL,uR) − cs, 0) and SR = max(max(uL,uR) + cs, 0). Clamping both speeds through zero is the trick that makes it cheap: SL ≤ 0 ≤ SR always holds, so the central formula is always the correct one, the supersonic branches disappear, and SR − SL ≥ 2cs means the division needs no guard. LLF (Rusanov) is one button away and cheaper still — a single speed max(|uL|,|uR|) + cs used symmetrically, at the price of more diffusion.
update
Unsplit and conservative: U += Δt/Δx [ (Fxi − Fxi+1) + (Fyj − Fyj+1) ]. All four faces of a cell in one pass, on a five-point stencil. Each face is solved twice, once for each cell that shares it — wasteful on a CPU, correct here, because a second pass to store the flux costs far more on this GPU than solving the Riemann problem again.
timestep
RAMSES's cmpdt, verbatim: ctot = Σdim(|udim| + cs), so in 2D |u| + |v| + 2cs; then Δt = C Δx / ctot, with a ceiling at C Δx / csmall to guard against ctot → 0. The sum of the directional speeds rather than max(|u| + cs), because the update is unsplit. C = 0.8, the RAMSES default, and no safety margin on top of it.

The part that matters is when: it is recomputed from the current state before every step, the way courant_fine runs ahead of amr_step. The maximum is reduced on the GPU into a single texel and read by the solver as a texture rather than a uniform, so there is no readback and no pipeline stall in the loop. That costs three extra passes per step and it is what makes the thing robust — see below.
precision
Single, throughout — which is less a choice than the only option: WebGL2's highp float is IEEE binary32 and there is no double path at all. The state textures are RGBA32F to match, three channels of four used. GLSL ES has no -ffast-math switch either, so that intent is written into the code instead: reciprocal multiplies rather than divides, no square root anywhere in the solver (an isothermal sound speed is a uniform), and a branch-free Riemann solver courtesy of those clamped wave speeds.
provenance
This is the scheme from mini-ramses-ism's GPU hydro path (gpu_hydro.cuf) — the same Riemann solver, the same wave-speed estimate, the same floors in the same places, the same Courant condition — reduced to two dimensions and to its cheapest configuration. What is missing relative to the reference is the AMR, the MHD, the constrained transport, and the slope/trace machinery that piecewise-constant reconstruction makes unnecessary.

Why the histogram matters

For isothermal supersonic turbulence the density PDF is log-normal, and its width obeys σ² = ln(1 + b²ℳ²), where b depends on how the turbulence is driven: about 1/3 for purely solenoidal forcing, about 1 for purely compressive. I want to be straight about how much of that this page actually demonstrates, because the answer is less than the plot implies.

What it does demonstrate: the distribution is log-normal in shape, and it gets there on its own from a uniform initial condition. Nothing in the code knows what a log-normal is.

What it does not demonstrate: the value of b. Measured σ from a single snapshot of this box scatters by tens of per cent between settings — I ran solenoidal, mixed and compressive for sixteen seconds each and got σ = 1.87, 1.16, 1.42, which is not even monotonic in the mixture. That is not the solver being wrong; it is σ being a second moment of a heavy-tailed field sampled over about three box crossing times, which is nowhere near enough. The readout now carries a running mean for that reason, and it still should not be read to better than a factor. On top of that, σ² = ln(1 + b²ℳ²) is calibrated on three-dimensional simulations — compression in 2D makes filaments where 3D makes sheets — and the b(mixture) curve here is a straight interpolation between the two known limits rather than a measured calibration. Three sources of slop, any one of which would spoil a factor-level comparison.

The check that is airtight is the one in the readout marked mass error. A conservative scheme differences fluxes across shared faces, so what leaves one cell enters its neighbour exactly, and total mass must hold to round-off. Over twenty thousand steps it drifts by about 5 × 10⁻⁸ relative — which is float32 round-off accumulating, and nothing else. That is the claim in the update row above, tested rather than asserted.

The shocked volume readout is the fraction of the volume converging faster than a fifth of the sound speed per cell. In a supersonically driven isothermal box that fraction is small — the gas spends most of its volume in rarefied regions and most of its mass in thin shocked sheets. That asymmetry is the whole reason star formation is inefficient.

The bug that taught me the timestep

For a while this page would, after a few minutes, develop an instability that swept across the box in about a second and left it uniform. Both halves of that had a cause and neither was the Riemann solver.

The sweep was a violated CFL condition. Δt was being sized from a maximum signal speed read back to the CPU once every sixteenth step. At high Mach the rarefactions in an isothermal box drive a sixth of the cells onto the density floor, and a floor on ρ is not a floor on u = m/ρ — so the true signal speed could multiply several-fold inside one measurement interval while Δt still reflected the old value. An explicit scheme stepping past its CFL limit grows error at the grid scale and spreads it about a cell per step, which across 460 cells is roughly the second it appeared to take.

The uniform box afterwards was my own fault twice over: a guard that called the initial conditions whenever the signal speed looked absurd. So the instability was real and the erasure was me, which is a bad combination — it destroyed the evidence every time.

Two wrong fixes came first, and they are worth naming. I added a safety margin to Δt; the margin was compensating for staleness rather than removing it. Then I scaled that margin by the growth in signal speed between measurements, which was worse than the bug: ordinary fluctuation reads as growth, so Δt was throttled almost permanently and the turbulence never developed at all — σ collapsed from about 1.4 to 0.1. A dead box is not a stable one.

The actual fix was to stop guessing and do what RAMSES does: compute the Courant condition from the current state before every step. Measured over the same abuse — Mach 12 driving plus sustained detonations and fast strokes — peak |u| went from 79 to 30, peak ctot from 105 to 37, with no velocity limiter anywhere and no restarts. The guard now fires only on a genuinely non-finite state, and when it does it says so in the readout, because a solver that silently restarts itself is worse than one that visibly does.

What it is not

No magnetic field, so no Alfvén waves, no magnetic pressure support, and no chance of showing the things that actually set the ISM's density structure at low Mach number. No self-gravity, so nothing collapses. No cooling function, because isothermal is the cooling function. No AMR. Two dimensions, so the vortex dynamics are wrong in the way two dimensions are always wrong. It is a demonstration of a scheme, not a science run — the real ones live in the papers.
